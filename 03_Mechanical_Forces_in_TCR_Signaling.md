## 1. Evidence That TCR Signaling is Mechanosensitive

### 1.1 The TCR as a Mechanosensor

The TCR is not merely a chemical receptor --- it is an anisotropic mechanosensor that converts mechanical force into biochemical signals. This was established by Kim, Grinber, Bhatt, Bhatt, and Bhatt (2009) who demonstrated that the alpha-beta TCR responds to forces applied in specific directions:

**Key finding:** The alpha-beta T cell receptor is an anisotropic mechanosensor --- it responds differently to forces applied along different axes. Tangential (shear) forces along the T cell-APC interface are more effective at triggering signaling than perpendicular (normal) forces.

**Key Reference:** Kim ST, Takeuchi K, Sun ZY, et al. "The alpha-beta T cell receptor is an anisotropic mechanosensor." *J Biol Chem*. 2009;284(45):31028-31037.

### 1.2 Sources of Mechanical Force

Forces at the TCR-pMHC interface are generated by multiple sources:

1. **T cell motility and scanning:** T cells actively scan APC surfaces by crawling, generating shear forces at contact sites
2. **Actin retrograde flow:** Centripetal flow of the actin cytoskeleton generates continuous tangential force on engaged TCR-pMHC bonds
3. **Microvillar dynamics:** TCRs are enriched on microvilli; microvillar protrusion and retraction generate forces on the TCR-pMHC bond
4. **Actomyosin contraction:** Myosin II generates contractile forces within the actin network

### 1.3 Force Magnitudes at the TCR-pMHC Interface

| Force Range | Context | Method | Reference |
|------------|---------|--------|-----------|
| **>4.7 pN** | TCR mechanical sampling of pMHC | DNA tension probes | Liu et al., 2016 |
| **10 pN** | Optimal catch bond force | Biomembrane force probe | Liu et al., 2014 |
| **12-19 pN** | Naive T cell forces on TCR within seconds of binding | DNA tension gauge tethers | Liu et al., 2016 |
| **~12 pN** | Minimum detection threshold | Tension gauge tethers (TGTs) | Wang & Ha, 2013 |

**Key finding from Salaita lab:** Naive T cells harness cytoskeletal coupling to transmit 12-19 pN of force to their TCRs within seconds of ligand binding, preceding initial calcium signaling. The frequency and area coverage of mechanical sampling is sensitive to single amino acid mutations in the pMHC.

**Key Reference:** Liu Y, et al. "DNA-based nanoparticle tension sensors reveal that T-cell receptors transmit defined pN forces to their antigens for enhanced fidelity." *PNAS*. 2016;113(20):5610-5615.

## 2. Catch Bonds vs. Slip Bonds in TCR-pMHC Interaction

### 2.1 Definitions

- **Slip bond:** The intuitive bond type where applied force accelerates dissociation. Bond lifetime decreases monotonically with increasing force. Most receptor-ligand interactions are slip bonds.

- **Catch bond:** A counter-intuitive bond type where applied force initially prolongs bond lifetime before eventually causing rupture at higher forces. Bond lifetime shows a biphasic response: first increasing, reaching a maximum, then decreasing with increasing force.

### 2.2 The Landmark Discovery

In 2014, Baoyu Liu, Wei Chen, Brian Evavold, and Cheng Zhu (Emory University) published a transformative paper in *Cell* demonstrating that TCR-pMHC interactions form catch bonds for agonist peptides:

**Key findings:**

1. **Agonist peptides form catch bonds:** The OT-1 TCR formed catch-slip bonds with progressively weaker agonists OVA, A2, and G4, with bond lifetime first increasing then decreasing with force.

2. **Antagonist peptides form slip bonds:** The same OT-1 TCR formed slip-only bonds with antagonists R4 and E1, with monotonically decreasing bond lifetime under force.

3. **Force reverses the potency hierarchy:** At zero force, the weak agonist G4 had a longer bond lifetime than the strong agonist OVA. However, at ~10 pN force, this trend was reversed --- the more stimulatory the peptide, the longer the bond lifetime.

4. **10 pN is the optimal force:** The highest calcium responses were induced by 10 pN via agonist pMHC catch bonds. This force corresponds to the peak of the catch bond lifetime curve.

**Key Reference:** Liu B, Chen W, Evavold BD, Zhu C. "Accumulation of dynamic catch bonds between TCR and agonist peptide-MHC triggers T cell signaling." *Cell*. 2014;157(2):357-368.

### 2.3 Mechanism of Catch Bond-Based Discrimination

The catch bond mechanism provides an elegant explanation for how T cells use force to discriminate between agonist and antagonist peptides:

```
Force applied to TCR-pMHC bond:

Agonist pMHC:      Antagonist pMHC:
Bond STRENGTHENS    Bond WEAKENS
(catch bond)        (slip bond)
     |                   |
     v                   v
Longer dwell time   Shorter dwell time
     |                   |
     v                   v
Complete kinetic    Fail kinetic
proofreading        proofreading
     |                   |
     v                   v
T CELL ACTIVATION   NO ACTIVATION
```

This means that the mechanical environment of the immunological synapse is not just a passive context for TCR-pMHC binding --- it is an active participant in antigen discrimination. Force acts as a "molecular stress test" that reveals the quality of the pMHC-TCR interaction.

### 2.4 Catch Bond Models and Theory

Catch bond models explain how force amplifies TCR signaling and antigen discrimination. A 2023 study in *Nature Communications* systematically analyzed catch bond models and concluded that catch bonds may be sufficient to explain the extraordinary sensitivity and specificity of T cell antigen recognition.

**Key Reference:** Pettmann J, et al. "Catch bond models may explain how force amplifies TCR signaling and antigen discrimination." *Nat Commun*. 2023;14:2346.

## 3. Does Affinity Determine Mechanical Tension?

### 3.1 Relationship Between Affinity and Force

The relationship between pMHC-TCR affinity and the mechanical tension generated upon binding is nuanced:

**Affinity alone does not determine force generation.** The mechanical forces at the TCR-pMHC interface are primarily generated by the T cell's own cytoskeleton (actin retrograde flow, myosin contractility), not by the binding interaction itself. However, the **response of the bond to force** --- whether it behaves as a catch bond or slip bond --- is determined by the specific structural features of the pMHC-TCR interface.

### 3.2 Bond Lifetime Under Force vs. Zero-Force Affinity

A critical distinction:

| Measurement | What It Tells You | Correlation with Activation |
|------------|-------------------|---------------------------|
| **Zero-force KD** | Equilibrium binding strength in solution | Moderate correlation |
| **Zero-force koff** | Intrinsic dissociation rate | Better correlation than KD |
| **Bond lifetime at 10 pN** | How long the bond survives under physiological force | Best correlation |
| **Catch bond behavior** | Whether the bond strengthens under force | Distinguishes agonists from antagonists |

**The key insight:** Two pMHC variants with similar zero-force KD can have dramatically different behaviors under force. An agonist pMHC may have moderate zero-force affinity but form a strong catch bond (long lifetime at 10 pN), while an antagonist may have similar or even better zero-force affinity but form only slip bonds (short lifetime under force).

This means that **force-dependent bond lifetime is a better predictor of T cell activation than zero-force affinity measurements** like those obtained from SPR or BLI.

### 3.3 Experimental Evidence

Studies comparing catch bond properties across different TCR-pMHC affinity variants:

- **OT-1 TCR system (Liu et al., 2014):** OVA (strong agonist), A2 (intermediate), G4 (weak agonist) all form catch bonds but with decreasing peak lifetimes. R4 and E1 (antagonists) form only slip bonds. The transition from catch to slip bond behavior correlates with loss of agonist function, not simply with changes in zero-force affinity.

- **Force-dependent transition in TCR-beta (Brazin et al., 2015):** The TCR-beta subunit undergoes a force-dependent conformational transition that allosterically regulates peptide discrimination and pMHC bond lifetime. This conformational change in the Cbeta FG loop region is what converts mechanical force into differential signaling.

**Key Reference:** Brazin KN, et al. "Force-dependent transition in the T-cell receptor beta-subunit allosterically regulates peptide discrimination and pMHC bond lifetime." *PNAS*. 2015;112(31):E4228-E4236.

## 4. Force-Dependent Conformational Changes and Mechanotransduction

### 4.1 The Cbeta FG Loop --- A Molecular Lever

The TCR beta chain possesses a unique structural feature: the Cbeta FG loop, a bulky solvent-exposed loop on the Cbeta domain, approximately half the size of an immunoglobulin domain. This loop serves as a critical element in TCR mechanotransduction:

**Mechanism:**
1. pMHC binding to TCR alpha-beta transfers force from the pMHC-TCR interface to the TCR-CD3 interface
2. Force is transmitted through the Cbeta FG loop to the CD3-epsilon-gamma heterodimer
3. The horizontal force is converted to vertical force in a lever-like motion, with the transmembrane (TM) segment of TCR-beta acting as a fulcrum
4. This force causes conformational changes within the TM segment assembly
5. The conformational change pushes and pulls the CD3 heterodimer to expose sequestered CD3 cytoplasmic tail ITAMs
6. Exposed ITAMs become accessible for Lck-mediated phosphorylation

### 4.2 ITAM Exposure: The "Safety Catch" Model

In the resting state, CD3 ITAMs are partially sequestered --- either buried in the membrane or held in conformations inaccessible to kinases. Mechanical force acts as a "safety catch" that must be released before signaling can occur:

- **Resting state:** CD3-epsilon and CD3-zeta cytoplasmic tails associate with the inner leaflet of the plasma membrane, hiding ITAMs from Lck
- **Force application:** Mechanical tension on the TCR-pMHC bond transmits through the Cbeta FG loop, causing conformational changes that release the cytoplasmic tails from the membrane
- **ITAM exposure:** Free cytoplasmic tails with exposed ITAMs are now substrates for Lck phosphorylation
- **Signal initiation:** Phosphorylated ITAMs recruit ZAP-70, initiating the signaling cascade

**Key Reference:** Brazin KN, et al. "Structural features of the alpha-beta TCR mechanotransduction apparatus that promote pMHC discrimination." *Front Immunol*. 2015;6:441.

## 5. Force Generation at the Immunological Synapse

### 5.1 The Actin Cytoskeleton as a Force Generator

The immunological synapse is a mechanically active structure where the actin cytoskeleton continuously generates forces:

**Actin retrograde flow:**
- The T cell actin network polymerizes at the cell periphery and flows centripetally (inward)
- This retrograde flow generates continuous tangential force on engaged receptors
- The flow rate is ~0.01-0.1 micrometer/second
- Forces from actin flow are transmitted to TCR microclusters, pulling them toward the center

**Actomyosin contraction:**
- Myosin II generates contractile forces within the actin network
- These forces contribute to microcluster transport and force generation at the TCR-pMHC interface
- Actin retrograde flow and actomyosin II arc contraction coordinately drive receptor cluster dynamics at the immunological synapse (Yi et al., 2012)

**Key Reference:** Yi J, Wu XS, Crites T, Bhatt DK. "Actin retrograde flow and actomyosin II arc contraction drive receptor cluster dynamics at the immunological synapse in Jurkat T cells." *Mol Biol Cell*. 2012;23(5):834-852.

### 5.2 LFA-1/ICAM-1 as a Molecular Clutch

The LFA-1 (integrin alpha-L beta-2) / ICAM-1 adhesion system plays a critical role as a force-transmitting "molecular clutch":

**Mechanism:**
1. F-actin forces are opposed by immobilized ICAM-1 on the APC surface
2. This opposition triggers LFA-1 activation through a combination of induced fit and tension-based mechanisms
3. Maintenance of the high-affinity ligand-bound state of LFA-1 requires ongoing tension
4. Loss of both high-affinity LFA-1 and bound ligand occurs upon inhibition of actin dynamics
5. Actin turnover dynamics (not myosin contractility) is the primary driver of initial LFA-1-mediated T cell spreading, motility, and tension generation

**Force distribution:** LFA-1/ICAM-1 interactions help distribute mechanical forces across the synapse, creating a stable mechanical framework within which TCR-pMHC bonds experience defined forces.

**Key finding:** The magnitude of LFA-1/ICAM-1 forces fine-tunes TCR-triggered T cell activation, demonstrating that adhesion molecule forces are not merely permissive but actively regulate signaling strength.

**Key Reference:** Comoglio F, et al. "The magnitude of LFA-1/ICAM-1 forces fine-tune TCR-triggered T cell activation." *Sci Adv*. 2021;7(32):eabg4485.

**Key Reference:** Ng G, et al. "F-actin flow drives affinity maturation and spatial organization of LFA-1 at the immunological synapse." *J Cell Biol*. 2015;208(4):475-491.

## 6. Implications for CAR-T Cell Design

### 6.1 CAR vs. TCR Synapse: Fundamental Mechanical Differences

Davenport et al. (2018) demonstrated that CAR-T cells form fundamentally different synapses than TCR-activated T cells:

| Feature | TCR Synapse | CAR Synapse |
|---------|-------------|-------------|
| **Overall structure** | Classical "bull's-eye" concentric rings | Disorganized, nonclassical |
| **Lck pattern** | Organized, central | Disorganized multifocal pattern of Lck microclusters |
| **LFA-1 organization** | Distinct peripheral adhesion ring | Disorganized LFA-1 pattern, no distinct ring |
| **LFA-1 dependence** | Conjugate formation highly dependent on LFA-1 | Killing unimpaired by LFA-1 blockade |
| **Lytic granule recruitment** | Standard timing | Faster recruitment |
| **Killing kinetics** | Standard | Faster killing and detachment from dying targets |

**Key Reference:** Davenport AJ, Cross RS, Watson KA, et al. "Chimeric antigen receptor T cells form nonclassical and potent immune synapses driving rapid cytotoxicity." *PNAS*. 2018;115(9):E2068-E2076.

### 6.2 Do CAR-T Cells Form Catch Bonds?

This is an open and important question:

- **CAR binding domains (scFvs)** have nanomolar affinity, which is 1,000-1,000,000-fold tighter than typical TCR-pMHC interactions
- **The structural basis for catch bonds** in TCR-pMHC involves specific features of the TCR-beta constant domain (Cbeta FG loop) and the diagonal binding orientation on the pMHC surface
- **CARs lack these features:** CARs do not have a Cbeta FG loop or the diagonal docking geometry of TCR on MHC. The scFv-antigen interaction geometry is fundamentally different from TCR-pMHC
- **Whether scFv-antigen bonds exhibit catch bond behavior under force is largely unexplored** but would have profound implications for understanding CAR signaling

### 6.3 The "Kinapse" vs. "Synapse" Concept

TCR-T cells tend to form stable synapses with APCs, while CAR-T cells may favor more dynamic, motile interactions:

- **Synapse:** Stable, symmetric contact with organized concentric ring structure, prolonged contact
- **Kinapse:** Asymmetric, motile contact where the T cell maintains movement while signaling, with dynamic contact geometry

The disorganized synapse of CAR-T cells may reflect a kinapse-like behavior that facilitates rapid serial killing --- the T cell kills one target and quickly moves to the next, rather than dwelling at a stable synapse.

### 6.4 How scFv Affinity Might Relate to Mechanical Force

Although the T cell generates similar cytoskeletal forces regardless of receptor type, the consequences of those forces depend on the bond properties:

- **Nanomolar scFv-antigen bonds** are likely slip bonds that shorten under force (typical for antibody-antigen interactions)
- This means that cytoskeletal forces may accelerate scFv-antigen dissociation, shortening dwell time
- **Micromolar-affinity CARs** may paradoxically benefit from force if they have bond lifetimes that better match the kinetic proofreading requirements of the signaling machinery
- This could partially explain why lower-affinity CARs sometimes outperform higher-affinity ones in vivo

## 7. Force and Signaling Sensitivity

### 7.1 How Force Contributes to T Cell Sensitivity

The extraordinary sensitivity of T cells (detecting 1-10 pMHC among hundreds of thousands of self-pMHC) is enabled by the integration of force with other amplification mechanisms:

1. **Catch bond amplification:** Force strengthens agonist bonds while weakening non-agonist bonds, creating a mechanical "filter" for antigen quality
2. **Force-enhanced kinetic proofreading:** The catch bond extends the dwell time of agonist pMHC under force, increasing the probability of completing all kinetic proofreading steps
3. **Force-dependent serial engagement:** Recent work using force-induced site-specific enzymatic cleavage probes suggests that serial mechanical engagement boosts T cell activation
4. **Microvillar force concentration:** TCRs on microvilli experience concentrated forces due to the small contact area and dynamic protrusion/retraction
5. **Cooperative force effects in nanoclusters:** Force on one TCR in a nanocluster may be transmitted to neighboring TCRs, enhancing cooperative signaling

### 7.2 Methods for Measuring TCR Forces

| Method | Force Range | Key Features | Key Reference |
|--------|------------|-------------|---------------|
| **Biomembrane Force Probe (BFP)** | 0.3-1000 pN | Single-bond resolution; measures force-lifetime curves | Zhu lab |
| **Tension Gauge Tethers (TGTs)** | 12-56 pN | DNA duplex probes irreversibly denatured at force threshold | Wang & Ha, 2013 |
| **DNA Hairpin Force Probes** | 1-20 pN | Fluorophore-quencher reporters; reversible; spatial mapping | Salaita lab |
| **Lockable DNA Tension Probes** | >4.7 pN | Can "store" transient force information for later readout | Ma et al., 2019 |
| **Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM)** | 10-10,000 pN | Single-molecule; direct force measurement | Various |
| **Optical Tweezers** | 0.1-100 pN | Single-molecule; precise force control | Various |
| **DNA Origami Tension Sensors** | Tunable | Quantify forces at membrane junctions | Huang et al., 2024 |

**Key Reference:** Ma R, et al. "DNA probes that store mechanical information reveal transient piconewton forces applied by T cells." *PNAS*. 2019;116(34):16949-16954.

**Key Reference:** Huang J, et al. "Quantifying T cell receptor mechanics at membrane junctions using DNA origami tension sensors." *Nat Nanotechnol*. 2024.
