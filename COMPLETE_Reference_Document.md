# PART I: pMHC-TCR Binding Kinetics and Affinity Parameters

## 1. Fundamentals of pMHC-TCR Binding Affinity

The interaction between the T cell receptor (TCR) and peptide-major histocompatibility complex (pMHC) is the central molecular event in adaptive immunity. This interaction is characterized by remarkably weak affinity compared to typical antibody-antigen interactions, yet it drives exquisitely sensitive and specific immune responses.

### 1.1 Structural Basis of the Interaction

The TCR engages pMHC through six complementarity-determining region (CDR) loops --- three from the TCR alpha chain (CDR1alpha, CDR2alpha, CDR3alpha) and three from the TCR beta chain (CDR1beta, CDR2beta, CDR3beta). These loops have distinct roles:

- **CDR1 and CDR2 (germline-encoded):** Encoded by V gene segments, these loops primarily contact the conserved alpha-helical residues of the MHC molecule that flank the peptide-binding groove. CDR2beta and CDR1beta germline residues contribute affinity determinants for MHC alpha helix residues along the rim of the peptide-binding groove (Garcia et al., 2009; Rossjohn et al., 2015).

- **CDR3 (somatically rearranged):** Generated by V(D)J recombination, CDR3 loops are the most variable and primarily contact the peptide. CDR3 is the principal determinant of peptide specificity (Rossjohn et al., 2015).

This dual-recognition strategy --- germline-encoded MHC contact via CDR1/2 and peptide-specific contact via CDR3 --- ensures that TCRs are inherently biased toward MHC recognition while maintaining broad peptide specificity.

### 1.2 Thermodynamic Basis of Binding

TCR-pMHC binding thermodynamics reveal no single enthalpic or entropic "signature." Instead, enthalpy and entropy changes vary in a compensatory manner across different TCR-pMHC pairs (Ely et al., 2006; Armstrong & Baker, 2007):

- **Enthalpy contributions:** Generally favorable, reflecting hydrogen bonds and van der Waals contacts formed at the interface.
- **Entropy contributions:** Variable --- can be favorable or unfavorable. The flexibility of CDR loops, particularly CDR3, means that binding often involves ordering of disordered loops, which carries an entropic penalty.
- **Induced fit:** TCR CDR loops are flexible in the free state and become ordered upon binding through localized folding transitions. TCR-pMHC binding proceeds by a two-step mechanism in which the second step is an induced-fit process that is rate-determining for antigen recognition (Wu et al., 2002; Willcox et al., 1999).
- **Heat capacity changes (Delta-Cp):** Changes in heat capacity upon binding provide evidence for conformational differences between bound and free TCRs, reflecting changes in hydration at the interface.

**Key Reference:** Ely LK, Beddoe T, Clements CS, et al. "Thermodynamics of T cell receptor binding to peptide-MHC: Evidence for a general mechanism of molecular scanning." *PNAS*. 2006;96(20):11446-11451.

## 2. Kinetic Parameters of TCR-pMHC Interaction

### 2.1 Equilibrium Dissociation Constant (KD)

The KD represents the ratio of koff/kon and reflects the overall binding strength. For TCR-pMHC interactions:

| Parameter | Typical Range | Notes |
|-----------|--------------|-------|
| **Wild-type TCR-pMHC KD** | 1-100 microM | Measured by SPR |
| **Class I-restricted (CD8+ T cells)** | 13 +/- 11 microM (average) | Higher affinity than Class II |
| **Class II-restricted (CD4+ T cells)** | 52 +/- 33 microM (average) | Lower affinity than Class I |
| **Engineered high-affinity TCRs** | 30 nM to 26 pM | 100-1000 fold improvement |

The affinity difference between Class I and Class II-restricted TCRs is significant: CD8+ T cell TCRs bind approximately 4-fold more tightly than CD4+ T cell TCRs on average.

**Key Reference:** Stone JD, Chervin AS, Kranz DM. "T-cell receptor binding affinities and kinetics: impact on T-cell activity and specificity." *Immunology*. 2009;126(2):165-176.

**Key Reference:** Aleksic M, Liddy N, Sherlock D, et al. "Human TCR-Binding Affinity is Governed by MHC Class Restriction." *J Immunol*. 2007;178(9):5727-5734.

### 2.2 Association Rate (kon)

The association rate reflects how quickly the TCR engages pMHC. For TCR-pMHC interactions, kon values show considerable variability:

| TCR System | kon (M-1 s-1) | Reference |
|-----------|---------------|-----------|
| Typical range | 600 - 400,000 | Stone et al., 2009 |
| 2C TCR / SIY-Kb | ~20,000 | Stone et al., 2009 |
| Representative agonists | 1,000 - 50,000 | Various SPR studies |

Importantly, kon values for TCR-pMHC interactions are relatively slow compared to many receptor-ligand systems. The 3D TCR-pMHC on-rates are more than 200-fold slower than, for example, the P-selectin/PSGL-1 on-rate (Huang et al., 2010). This slow association rate is thought to reflect the conformational adjustment (induced fit) required for productive binding.

### 2.3 Dissociation Rate (koff) --- The Key Discriminatory Parameter

The dissociation rate is widely considered the most important kinetic parameter for predicting T cell functional outcomes:

| Parameter | Typical Range | Significance |
|-----------|--------------|-------------|
| **koff** | 0.009 - 0.975 s-1 | Spans ~100-fold range |
| **Half-life (t1/2)** | <1 sec to >75 sec | For agonist ligands |

**Why koff matters more than KD:**

Multiple lines of evidence establish koff as the better predictor of T cell activation:

1. **TCR-ligand dissociation rate is a robust and stable biomarker of CD8+ T cell potency** --- substantial correlations were observed between koff and calcium mobilization, target cell recognition, and expression of both costimulatory and coinhibitory receptors (Hebeisen et al., 2015).

2. **Altered peptide ligand studies** demonstrated that the dissociation rate determines the agonist and antagonist properties of pMHC ligands. Single amino acid substitutions in peptides that alter koff, even when KD remains similar, dramatically change T cell responses (Matsui et al., 1994; Corr et al., 1994).

3. **Mathematical modeling** shows that except in the pure occupancy model, predicted T cell responses depend on the individual values of kon and koff rather than on their quotient (KD), allowing systems with the same KD to show quite different responses (Dushek et al., 2019).

**Key Reference:** Hebeisen M, Allard M, Gannon PO, et al. "Identifying Individual T Cell Receptors of Optimal Avidity for Tumor Antigens." *Front Immunol*. 2015;6:582.

**Key Reference:** Hebeisen M, Schmidt J, Guillaume P, et al. "TCR-ligand dissociation rate is a robust and stable biomarker of CD8+ T cell potency." *JCI Insight*. 2017;2(14):e92570.

## 3. The Kinetic Proofreading Model

### 3.1 McKeithan's Model (1995)

Timothy McKeithan proposed that the TCR signal transduction cascade functions as a kinetic proofreading mechanism, analogous to the proofreading that ensures fidelity in DNA replication and protein synthesis.

**Core mechanism:** After pMHC binding, the TCR-CD3 complex undergoes multiple sequential modification steps (primarily tyrosine phosphorylation events) before a signal is transmitted. Full T cell activation requires the TCR-pMHC interaction to persist long enough to complete all necessary modification steps. If the pMHC dissociates before completion, the modifications revert to the unmodified state, and no signal is produced.

**Key features:**
- Each sequential step acts as a "checkpoint" --- the pMHC must remain bound through each step
- N sequential steps amplify minor differences in koff exponentially (by a factor of approximately koff^N)
- This explains how T cells discriminate between self-peptides (fast koff, short dwell time) and foreign peptides (slow koff, longer dwell time) even when KD differences are modest

**Key Reference:** McKeithan TW. "Kinetic proofreading in T-cell receptor signal transduction." *Proc Natl Acad Sci USA*. 1995;92(11):5042-5046.

### 3.2 Experimental Validation

Recent work has provided direct experimental evidence for kinetic proofreading in TCR signaling. Torigoe et al. (2022) demonstrated that kinetic proofreading through multi-step activation of ZAP70 kinase underlies early T cell ligand discrimination, with progressive enhancement of discrimination from receptor proximal (TCR phosphorylation) to more distal signaling events (DAG generation).

**Key Reference:** Torigoe C, et al. "Kinetic proofreading through the multi-step activation of the ZAP70 kinase underlies early T cell ligand discrimination." *Nat Immunol*. 2022;23:1045-1056.

## 4. The Optimal Dwell Time Concept

### 4.1 The "Goldilocks" Principle

Efficient T cell activation requires an optimal dwell time of TCR-pMHC interaction --- neither too short nor too long:

- **Too short (koff too fast):** The pMHC dissociates before kinetic proofreading is complete. No productive signaling occurs.
- **Too long (koff too slow):** The pMHC remains bound to one TCR, preventing serial engagement of multiple TCRs. This limits signal amplification.
- **Optimal (intermediate koff):** The pMHC remains bound long enough for productive signaling but dissociates in time to engage additional TCRs.

### 4.2 Quantitative Evidence

Kalergis et al. (2001) provided key evidence for the optimal dwell time concept:

- TCR-pMHC complexes with **low half-life (less than or equal to 10.3 seconds)** failed to induce efficient killing
- Complexes with **high half-life (77 seconds)** also showed impaired function
- Only **intermediate half-life (34 seconds)** TCR-pMHC interactions induced efficient cytotoxic granule secretion and tumor killing by CD8+ T cells in vivo

This demonstrates that T cell activation follows a bell-shaped curve when plotted against dwell time, rather than a simple monotonic increase with affinity.

**Key Reference:** Kalergis AM, Boucheron N, Cardenas MA, et al. "Efficient T cell activation requires an optimal dwell-time of interaction between the TCR and the pMHC complex." *Nat Immunol*. 2001;2(3):229-234.

**Key Reference:** Colf LA, et al. "The Goldilocks Model for TCR --- Too Much Attraction Might Not Be Best for Vaccine Design." *PLoS Biol*. 2010;8(9):e1000482.

## 5. Methods for Measuring TCR-pMHC Affinity and Kinetics

### 5.1 Three-Dimensional (3D/Solution) Methods

#### Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR)
- **Principle:** Measures mass-dependent changes in refractive index when molecules bind to a sensor surface
- **Platform:** Biacore (Cytiva) is the gold standard
- **Outputs:** KD, kon, koff in 3D solution phase
- **Advantages:** High sensitivity, real-time kinetics, label-free
- **Limitations:** One binding partner must be immobilized; may not reflect membrane-constrained binding

#### Bio-Layer Interferometry (BLI)
- **Principle:** Measures interference patterns in reflected light from biosensor tip as molecules bind
- **Platform:** Octet (Sartorius/ForteBio)
- **Outputs:** KD, kon, koff
- **Advantages:** No microfluidics required, simpler operation, higher throughput than SPR, can use unpurified samples/cell lysates
- **Limitations:** Lower sensitivity than SPR for very weak interactions; one partner immobilized

**Both SPR and BLI are orthogonal methods** --- measuring the same attribute with different detection principles, providing independent validation.

### 5.2 Two-Dimensional (2D/Membrane) Methods

#### Micropipette Adhesion Frequency Assay
- **Principle:** Measures adhesion frequency between a T cell and an antigen-presenting red blood cell as a function of contact time and area
- **Outputs:** Effective 2D affinity (AcKa, in micrometer^4), 2D koff
- **Key finding:** 2D measurements reveal dramatically different kinetics than 3D measurements

#### Why 2D and 3D Measurements Differ

Huang et al. (2010) made the landmark discovery that 2D kinetics are fundamentally different from 3D kinetics:

| Parameter | 3D (SPR) | 2D (Micropipette) |
|-----------|----------|-------------------|
| **koff for agonists** | Slowest | Fastest (paradoxically) |
| **Dynamic range** | Narrow | Broad (matches T cell responses) |
| **Off-rate difference** | - | Up to 8,300-fold faster than 3D |
| **Correlation with function** | Moderate | Strong |

The critical insight: **In 2D, agonist pMHC dissociates the fastest** (opposite of 3D), and the 2D affinity and on-rate have far broader dynamic ranges that match the dynamic range of T cell responses. The high 2D affinities for agonist pMHCs were driven by rapid 2D on-rates.

**This suggests that 2D measurements may be more physiologically relevant** because they capture the membrane-constrained geometry of the actual T cell-APC interface.

**Key Reference:** Huang J, Zarnitsyna VI, Liu B, et al. "The kinetics of two-dimensional TCR and pMHC interactions determine T-cell responsiveness." *Nature*. 2010;464(7290):932-936.

### 5.3 Single-Molecule Methods

- **pMHC tetramer staining:** Indirect measure of TCR avidity (not pure affinity); influenced by TCR density and coreceptor contribution
- **NTAmer dissociation assay:** Allows quantitative assessment of TCR-pMHC dissociation rates on living T cells; NTAmers can be dissociated by imidazole addition, enabling real-time koff measurement (Hebeisen et al., 2015)

## 6. Implications for CAR-T Cell Design

### 6.1 The Affinity Mismatch Problem

A fundamental difference between natural TCR-pMHC interactions and CAR-antigen interactions is the orders-of-magnitude affinity difference:

| Receptor Type | Typical KD Range | Notes |
|--------------|-----------------|-------|
| **TCR for pMHC** | 1-100 microM | Micromolar |
| **scFv in CAR** | 0.1-10 nM | Nanomolar |
| **Difference** | 1,000 - 1,000,000 fold | CARs bind much tighter |

This supraphysiological affinity has significant consequences:

1. **Reduced serial killing:** The unnaturally slow off-rate of nanomolar-affinity scFvs deviates from the transient, dynamic interactions found between TCRs and pMHCs. High affinity and avidity interactions by CAR can reduce the T cell's propensity for serial killing, potentially causing exhaustion or increased susceptibility to activation-induced cell death.

2. **Off-tumor toxicity:** Nanomolar-affinity CARs may recognize low levels of antigen on normal tissues, causing on-target/off-tumor toxicity.

3. **Exhaustion susceptibility:** T cells expressing traditional nanomolar affinity CARs may operate sub-optimally and be more prone to exhaustion and excessive cytokine release.

### 6.2 Evidence That Lower CAR Affinity Can Be Superior

Multiple studies demonstrate that reducing scFv affinity can paradoxically improve CAR-T cell therapeutic outcomes:

- **Ghorashian et al. (2019):** CAT-CAR T cells using a CAT scFv with 40-fold lower affinity for CD19 than FMC63 showed greater in vitro antigen-specific cytotoxicity and proliferation, confirmed in vivo. Published in *Nature Reviews Clinical Oncology* commentary: "CD19 affinity --- is lower also better?"

- **Park et al. (2017):** Micromolar affinity CAR T cells targeting ICAM-1 achieved rapid tumor elimination while avoiding systemic toxicity in a solid tumor mouse model, with superior anti-tumor efficacy and safety compared to nanomolar counterparts.

- **Mechanism:** The moderate-affinity CARs have similar kon but much higher koff compared to high-affinity FMC63, which may contribute to the formation of memory T cells and polyfunctionality. The dwell time and kinetics of antigen-binding domain engagement may be more important than KD in deciding CAR-T outcomes.

**Key Reference:** Park S, Shevlin E, Bhutani D, et al. "Micromolar affinity CAR T cells to ICAM-1 achieves rapid tumor elimination while avoiding systemic toxicity." *Sci Rep*. 2017;7:14366.

**Key Reference:** Ghorashian S, et al. discussed in: Brudno JN, Kochenderfer JN. "CD19 affinity --- is lower also better?" *Nat Rev Clin Oncol*. 2019;16(5):278-1.

# PART II: Self vs Non-Self: TCR Affinity Windows and Implications

## 1. TCR Affinity Windows in Thymic Selection

### 1.1 The Thymic Selection Paradigm

T cell development in the thymus involves a quality control process that selects for TCRs within a specific affinity window for self-pMHC:

- **No/negligible binding (death by neglect):** Thymocytes whose TCRs fail to bind any self-pMHC die by apoptosis (~95% of all thymocytes)
- **Weak-to-moderate binding (positive selection):** TCRs that bind self-pMHC with sufficient but not excessive affinity receive survival signals and mature
- **Strong binding (negative selection):** TCRs that bind self-pMHC too strongly are deleted (clonal deletion) or diverted to regulatory T cell lineage

### 1.2 Quantitative Affinity Thresholds

Based on verified experimental data:

| Selection Outcome | KD Range | Key Evidence |
|------------------|----------|-------------|
| **Positive selection** | ~100-300 microM | Catnb-Kb (KD ~136 microM) and Cappa1-Kb (KD ~211 microM) promote positive selection of OT-1 thymocytes |
| **Transition zone** | ~20-60 microM | Intermediate affinity; outcome depends on co-receptor (CD4 vs CD8) and avidity |
| **Negative selection** | < ~6-10 microM | OVA-Kb agonist (KD ~8.7 microM) drives negative selection of OT-1 thymocytes |
| **"Danger zone"** | ~6 microM | Approximately the negative selection threshold for autoantigens |

**Key quantitative comparisons from the OT-1 system:**
- Positively selecting peptides: Catnb-Kb has 15.6-fold lower affinity than OVA-Kb; Cappa1-Kb has 24.3-fold lower affinity
- This demonstrates a clear quantitative gap between the affinity of positively selecting (self) and negatively selecting (agonist foreign) ligands

**Key Reference:** Juang J, Ebert PJR, Feng D, et al. "Peptide-MHC heterodimers show that thymic positive selection requires a more restricted set of self-peptides than negative selection." *J Exp Med*. 2010;207(6):1223-1234.

**Key Reference:** Contan C, et al. "Affinity of thymic self-peptides for the TCR determines the selection of CD8+ T lymphocytes in the thymus." *Int Immunol*. 2000;12(9):1353-1364.

### 1.3 CD4 vs. CD8 T Cell Selection Differences

The affinity window differs between CD4+ and CD8+ T cells:

| Parameter | CD8+ T cells (Class I) | CD4+ T cells (Class II) |
|-----------|----------------------|----------------------|
| **Average mature TCR KD for foreign Ag** | 13 +/- 11 microM | 52 +/- 33 microM |
| **Selection window** | Narrower, higher affinity | Broader, lower affinity |
| **CD8/CD4 coreceptor effect** | CD8 enhances binding, narrows window | CD4 provides less enhancement |

CD8+ T cells have TCRs with approximately 4-fold higher average affinity for pMHC than CD4+ T cells, reflecting differences in coreceptor contribution and the structural constraints of MHC Class I vs. Class II recognition.

**Key Reference:** Aleksic M, Liddy N, et al. "Human TCR-Binding Affinity is Governed by MHC Class Restriction." *J Immunol*. 2007;178(9):5727-5734.

## 2. Affinity of Mature T Cells for Foreign Antigens

### 2.1 Typical KD Ranges by Antigen Category

| Antigen Category | Typical KD Range | Notes |
|-----------------|-----------------|-------|
| **Viral antigens (acute)** | 1-50 microM | Generally higher affinity; robust negative selection removes only the strongest |
| **Viral antigens (chronic)** | 1-100 microM | Broader range; includes lower affinity clones |
| **Bacterial antigens** | 1-100 microM | Comparable to viral |
| **Tumor-associated antigens (self-derived)** | 50-200+ microM | Low affinity due to thymic tolerance |
| **Tumor neoantigens (mutated)** | 1-50 microM | Higher affinity; not subject to central tolerance |
| **Autoimmune TCRs (escaped tolerance)** | >100-200+ microM | Very low affinity |

### 2.2 Why Tumor Antigen-Specific T Cells Have Lower Affinity

Most tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) are derived from overexpressed or aberrantly expressed self-proteins (e.g., NY-ESO-1, MAGE-A3, gp100, MART-1). Because these peptides are presented in the thymus during T cell development:

1. **High-affinity TCRs are deleted:** T cells with strong binding to self-derived tumor peptides are eliminated by negative selection
2. **Only low-affinity escapees survive:** Peripheral T cells against tumor-associated self-peptides that escape negative selection exhibit lower average affinities --- mean KD values of approximately 100 microM compared to approximately 10 microM for viral antigens
3. **Functional consequence:** These low-affinity T cells often fail to mount effective anti-tumor responses without therapeutic intervention

**The "affinity gap" for tumor immunity:**
- Mean KD for viral antigen TCRs: ~10 microM
- Mean KD for tumor self-antigen TCRs: ~100 microM
- This 10-fold difference in affinity translates to significant differences in T cell functional potency

**Key Reference:** Hoffmann MM, Slansky JE. "T cell receptor affinity in the age of cancer immunotherapy." *Mol Carcinog*. 2020;59(7):862-870.

**Key Reference:** Zhong S, et al. "TCR affinity for p/MHC formed by tumor antigens that are self-proteins: impact on efficacy and toxicity." *Curr Opin Immunol*. 2015;33:16-22.

## 3. The Affinity Ceiling Concept

### 3.1 Beyond an Optimal Affinity, Function Declines

Increasing TCR affinity does not monotonically improve T cell function. Instead, there exists an "affinity ceiling" beyond which:

1. **Function plateaus:** Additional increases in binding strength do not further enhance cytotoxicity, cytokine production, or proliferation
2. **Function can decrease:** Supraphysiological affinity may actually impair T cell responses through:
   - Loss of peptide specificity (increased cross-reactivity)
   - Impaired serial engagement (too-slow off-rate prevents serial triggering)
   - Enhanced exhaustion susceptibility in chronic antigen settings
   - Activation-induced cell death

### 3.2 The MAGE-A3 Catastrophe: A Cautionary Tale

The dangers of supraphysiological TCR affinity were dramatically illustrated by the fatal outcome of an anti-MAGE-A3 engineered TCR clinical trial:

- An affinity-enhanced TCR targeting MAGE-A3 was engineered for adoptive T cell therapy
- The enhanced affinity broadened cross-reactivity to structurally similar peptides
- The engineered TCR cross-reacted with a peptide from the cardiac protein titin, expressed in heart muscle
- This resulted in fatal cardiac inflammation (cardiomyopathy)
- The tragedy underscored that affinity enhancement beyond the natural range carries risks of loss of specificity

**This demonstrates that the thymic selection window (1-100 microM) is not merely a developmental constraint but reflects an optimized range for balancing sensitivity and specificity.**

### 3.3 Optimal Affinity for Anti-Tumor Responses

Based on accumulating evidence, the optimal affinity for TCR-mediated anti-tumor immunity appears to be:

- **For tumor-associated self-antigens:** A KD of approximately 10 microM has been identified as the optimal affinity threshold --- strong enough for effective function but not so strong as to cause unacceptable autoimmunity
- **For engineered TCRs:** Enhanced-affinity murine TCRs for tumor/self-antigens can be safe despite surpassing the negative selection threshold, but there is a narrow window of safety
- **The "danger zone":** KD of approximately 6 microM, near the negative selection threshold, represents a zone where autoreactivity risk increases sharply

**Key Reference:** Zhong S, et al. "Different affinity windows for virus and cancer-specific T-cell receptors: implications for therapeutic strategies." *Eur J Immunol*. 2012;42(10):2532-2541.

## 4. How Affinity Differences Impact Signaling and Function

### 4.1 Cytokine Production

TCR affinity/koff directly impacts the quality and quantity of cytokine production:

| Affinity | IL-2 | IFN-gamma | TNF-alpha | Notes |
|---------|------|-----------|-----------|-------|
| **High (agonist)** | Strong | Strong | Strong | Full effector program |
| **Intermediate** | Moderate | Strong | Moderate | Biased toward cytotoxicity |
| **Low (weak agonist)** | Weak/absent | Weak | Weak | Partial activation |
| **Very low (antagonist)** | None | None | None | May inhibit activation |

Altered peptide ligands that change koff (even when KD is similar) dramatically alter the cytokine production profile, demonstrating that kinetics, not just equilibrium binding, determines functional output.

### 4.2 Proliferation and Clonal Expansion

- Higher affinity TCRs generally drive more robust clonal expansion
- However, very high-affinity interactions can impair proliferation through activation-induced cell death (AICD)
- In chronic antigen settings (tumors, chronic infections), intermediate-affinity TCRs may sustain proliferation better than high-affinity TCRs that exhaust rapidly

### 4.3 Memory Differentiation

TCR affinity influences the balance between effector and memory differentiation:

- **Higher affinity:** Biases toward terminal effector differentiation
- **Lower affinity:** May favor memory precursor differentiation
- This has important implications for durable anti-tumor immunity, where memory formation is critical for preventing relapse

### 4.4 Exhaustion Susceptibility

Higher affinity TCR-pMHC interactions correlate with increased exhaustion susceptibility in chronic antigen settings:

- High-affinity T cells in the tumor microenvironment show enhanced expression of exhaustion markers (PD-1, TIM-3, LAG-3)
- This is likely due to stronger and more sustained signaling that drives the exhaustion transcriptional program
- The paradox: the T cells with the "best" affinity for tumor antigens may exhaust the fastest

## 5. Implications for CAR-T and Engineered TCR Therapy

### 5.1 The scFv Affinity vs. TCR Affinity Mismatch

| Parameter | Natural TCR | CAR scFv | Ratio |
|-----------|------------|----------|-------|
| **Typical KD** | 1-100 microM | 0.1-10 nM | 1,000-1,000,000x |
| **Typical koff** | 0.01-1 s-1 | 0.001-0.01 s-1 | 10-100x slower for CAR |
| **Bond type under force** | Catch bond (agonist) | Likely slip bond | Fundamentally different |

This orders-of-magnitude difference in binding strength means that CARs operate in a fundamentally different signaling regime than natural TCRs:

- **Prolonged engagement:** The slow off-rate of nanomolar scFvs means each CAR-antigen interaction persists much longer than a TCR-pMHC interaction
- **Blocked serial engagement:** The prolonged dwell time may prevent serial triggering of multiple CARs by the same antigen molecule
- **Tonic signaling risk:** Even in the absence of antigen, nanomolar-affinity scFvs may mediate weak tonic signaling through low-level cross-linking

### 5.2 Evidence That Reducing CAR Affinity Improves Outcomes

Multiple studies demonstrate that lower-affinity CARs can outperform higher-affinity versions:

**Anti-CD19 CAR (Ghorashian et al.):**
- CAT scFv: 40-fold lower affinity for CD19 than FMC63
- CAT-CAR T cells showed greater in vitro antigen-specific cytotoxicity and proliferation
- Confirmed in vivo with enhanced anti-leukemic effect in pediatric patients
- CAT mAb has similar kon as FMC63 but much higher koff
- The faster off-rate may contribute to memory T cell formation and polyfunctionality
- Commentary: "CD19 affinity --- is lower also better?" (*Nat Rev Clin Oncol*, 2019)

**Anti-ICAM-1 CAR (Park et al., 2017):**
- Micromolar affinity CAR T cells achieved rapid tumor elimination while avoiding systemic toxicity
- Superior anti-tumor efficacy AND safety compared to nanomolar counterparts in a solid tumor mouse model
- Demonstrates that micromolar affinity CARs can function effectively

**Anti-HER2 CAR (Liu et al.):**
- Low-affinity CAR-T cells moved from liver to high HER2-expressing tumor faster than high-affinity cells
- May contribute to better anti-tumor efficacy by improving biodistribution

**The emerging principle:** The dwell time and kinetics of antigen-binding domain engagement may be more important than KD in deciding the outcome of CAR-T therapy. Moderate-affinity CARs may better recapitulate the kinetic regime that natural TCRs operate in.

**Key Reference:** Ghorashian S, et al. discussed in: "CD19 affinity --- is lower also better?" *Nat Rev Clin Oncol*. 2019;16(5):278-281.

**Key Reference:** Park S, et al. "Micromolar affinity CAR T cells to ICAM-1 achieves rapid tumor elimination while avoiding systemic toxicity." *Sci Rep*. 2017;7:14366.

### 5.3 The Affinity Optimization Window for CARs

Based on current evidence, an "affinity optimization window" for CARs is emerging:

```
                   ← Increasing Affinity →

Too Weak        Optimal Range       Too Strong
(>10 microM)    (~100 nM - 1 microM)    (<1 nM)
     |                |                    |
     v                v                    v
Poor target     Efficient killing     Tonic signaling
recognition     Good persistence      Early exhaustion
No killing      Memory formation      Off-tumor toxicity
                Reduced toxicity      Blocked serial killing
```

This window is not absolute and depends on:
- Antigen density on target cells
- CAR design (costimulatory domains)
- Target antigen expression on normal tissues
- The specific disease context

## 6. Measuring TCR Affinity: Methods and Caveats

### 6.1 Three-Dimensional Methods (Solution-Phase)

**Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR) --- Biacore:**
- Gold standard for 3D affinity measurement
- Provides KD, kon, koff
- Requires purified, soluble TCR and pMHC
- Limitation: Measures interactions in solution, not at cell membranes

**Bio-Layer Interferometry (BLI) --- Octet:**
- Label-free, real-time kinetics
- Higher throughput than SPR
- Can use unpurified samples and cell lysates (more native conditions)
- Orthogonal validation of SPR measurements

### 6.2 Two-Dimensional Methods (Membrane-Constrained)

**Micropipette Adhesion Frequency Assay:**
- Measures binding between receptors on live T cells and ligands on opposing cell/bead
- Provides 2D affinity (AcKa) and 2D koff
- Reveals dramatically different kinetics from 3D measurements (Huang et al., 2010)

**FRET-Based 2D Kinetics:**
- Measures TCR-pMHC binding and unbinding in real-time at cell-cell interfaces
- Provides 2D bond lifetimes

### 6.3 Indirect Affinity Measures

**pMHC Tetramer Staining:**
- Widely used in clinical and research settings
- Measures avidity (collective binding strength of multiple TCR-pMHC interactions)
- Influenced by TCR density, co-receptor contribution, and membrane organization
- Not a pure affinity measurement but correlates with it

**NTAmer Dissociation Assay:**
- Allows quantitative assessment of TCR-pMHC dissociation rates on living T cells
- NTAmers (reversibly multimeric pMHC) can be dissociated by imidazole
- Enables real-time koff measurement at the cell surface
- More physiologically relevant than solution-phase SPR

### 6.4 Why 2D and 3D Measurements Differ

The dramatic differences between 2D and 3D measurements arise from:

1. **Membrane confinement:** Restricting molecules to 2D membranes changes the encounter geometry and effective concentrations
2. **Molecular orientation:** Membrane anchoring constrains molecular orientation, affecting binding efficiency
3. **Co-receptors and accessory molecules:** 2D measurements capture the contribution of co-receptors (CD4/CD8) and adhesion molecules (LFA-1) that are absent in 3D SPR
4. **Mechanical forces:** 2D measurements at cell-cell interfaces include the effects of cytoskeletal forces on bond behavior

**The critical paradox:** In 2D, agonist pMHC dissociates the fastest (not the slowest), and the 2D on-rate, not the off-rate, is the best discriminator of agonist potency. This suggests that the "faster scanning and rebinding" of agonist pMHC, facilitated by rapid 2D on-rates, may be more important than prolonged individual bond lifetime.

# PART III: TCR Clustering and the Serial Engagement Model

## 1. The Serial Engagement Model

### 1.1 Original Discovery

The serial engagement (or serial triggering) model was proposed by Salvatore Valitutti, Stefan Muller, Marina Cella, Elisabetta Padovan, and Antonio Lanzavecchia in 1995, published in *Nature*.

**Core observation:** When T cells interacted with antigen-presenting cells (APCs) displaying low antigenic pMHC densities (approximately 100 pMHC per APC), a surprisingly large number of TCRs were triggered --- up to 18,000 per T cell. This was far more than the number of pMHC molecules available, implying that each pMHC molecule must sequentially engage and trigger multiple TCRs.

**Estimated serial engagement capacity:** A single pMHC complex can serially engage and trigger approximately **~200 TCRs** over the course of a T cell-APC interaction.

**Key Reference:** Valitutti S, Muller S, Cella M, Padovan E, Lanzavecchia A. "Serial triggering of many T-cell receptors by a few peptide-MHC complexes." *Nature*. 1995;375(6527):148-151. PMID: 7753171.

### 1.2 Mechanism of Serial Engagement

The serial engagement model operates as follows:

1. A pMHC molecule on the APC surface binds to a TCR on the T cell
2. The bound TCR initiates intracellular signaling (phosphorylation cascades)
3. After a characteristic dwell time (determined by koff), the pMHC dissociates from the triggered TCR
4. The now-free pMHC binds to a new, un-triggered TCR nearby
5. This cycle repeats, with the same pMHC molecule serially triggering multiple TCRs
6. The triggered TCRs are internalized and degraded (TCR downregulation)

**Time per engagement cycle:** The dwell time of individual TCR-pMHC interactions has half-lives measured in seconds (in 3D: 1-75 seconds depending on the pMHC; in 2D: substantially faster). Two-dimensional off-rates are up to 8,300-fold faster than those measured in 3D solution phase, with agonist pMHC dissociating the fastest in 2D (Huang et al., 2010).

### 1.3 Relationship with Kinetic Proofreading

The serial engagement model and the kinetic proofreading model (McKeithan, 1995) are complementary but create a tension:

- **Kinetic proofreading demands longer dwell time:** Each TCR must remain bound to pMHC long enough to complete multiple sequential phosphorylation steps.
- **Serial engagement demands shorter dwell time:** pMHC must dissociate quickly to engage more TCRs.

This creates the "optimal dwell time" (Goldilocks) window:

```
Too Short             Optimal              Too Long
|------ No signal ------|--- Max signal ---|--- Blocked serial engagement ---|
koff too fast           koff just right      koff too slow
(<< 1 sec dwell)        (~1-30 sec dwell)    (>> 30 sec dwell)
```

**Key evidence:** Kalergis et al. (2001) demonstrated that neither low (less than or equal to 10.3 sec) nor high (77 sec) half-life TCR-pMHC interactions, but only intermediate (34 sec) half-life interactions induced efficient cytotoxic function in vivo.

**Key Reference:** Kalergis AM, et al. "Efficient T cell activation requires an optimal dwell-time of interaction between the TCR and the pMHC complex." *Nat Immunol*. 2001;2(3):229-234.

### 1.4 Criticisms and Refinements

The serial engagement model, while influential, has faced several criticisms over its 30-year history:

1. **TCR downregulation overestimation:** Some of the original evidence relied on TCR downregulation as a proxy for triggering. However, TCR co-modulation (bystander downregulation of un-triggered TCRs) may cause overestimation of the number of serially engaged TCRs.

2. **2D vs 3D kinetics discrepancy:** The 2D off-rates measured on cell membranes are dramatically faster than 3D solution measurements, which complicates quantitative predictions of serial engagement rates.

3. **Mechanosensing challenges the model:** The discovery that TCR-pMHC forms catch bonds under force (Liu et al., 2014) suggests that force may prolong bond lifetimes beyond what solution kinetics predict, potentially reducing the rate of serial engagement.

4. **Single-molecule studies:** Direct single-molecule measurement of TCR triggering showed that individual agonist pMHC remain bound to the same TCR for at least several seconds (for the 5c.c7 TCR) and for approximately one minute (for the AND TCR) in living primary T cells (O'Donoghue et al., 2013).

**Key Reference:** Valitutti S. "The Serial Engagement Model 17 Years After: From TCR Triggering to Immunotherapy." *Front Immunol*. 2012;3:272.

**Key Reference:** O'Donoghue GP, et al. "Direct single molecule measurement of TCR triggering by agonist pMHC in living primary T cells." *eLife*. 2013;2:e00778.

## 2. TCR Microclusters

### 2.1 Discovery and Definition

TCR microclusters are small, discrete aggregations of TCR molecules and associated signaling proteins that form at the T cell-APC contact interface. They were first described in detail by Alfredo Campi, Rajat Varma, and Michael Dustin in seminal studies published in 2005-2006.

**Definition:** TCR microclusters are submicron-scale signaling units containing TCR, CD3 chains, and associated signaling molecules (ZAP-70, SLP-76, LAT) that form at the periphery of the immunological synapse and serve as the primary sites of TCR signal initiation.

**Key Reference:** Campi A, Varma R, Dustin ML. "Actin and agonist MHC-peptide complex-dependent T cell receptor microclusters as scaffolds for signaling." *J Exp Med*. 2005;202(8):1031-1036.

**Key Reference:** Varma R, Campi A, Yokosuka T, Dustin ML. "T cell receptor-proximal signals are sustained in peripheral microclusters and terminated in the central supramolecular activation cluster." *Immunity*. 2006;25(1):117-127.

### 2.2 Number of TCR Molecules per Microcluster

Quantitative measurements of TCR molecules per microcluster:

| Parameter | Value | Method | Reference |
|-----------|-------|--------|-----------|
| **TCR per microcluster** | ~50-300 | Fluorescence intensity | Yokosuka & Saito, 2010 |
| **Typical estimate** | ~100 TCR molecules | Fluorescence intensity calibration | Dustin et al., various |
| **Microcluster area** | 0.35-0.5 micrometer^2 | Microscopy | Various |
| **Microcluster diameter** | ~0.5-1 micrometer | TIRFM/confocal | Yokosuka et al., 2005 |

The number of TCRs within a microcluster remains remarkably constant regardless of ligand potency --- the size and TCR content of a microcluster are not a function of the pMHC potency. Instead, potency affects the number of microclusters formed and the signaling output per cluster (Crites et al., 2014).

**Key Reference:** Crites TJ, Padhan K, Muller J, et al. "TCR Microclusters Preexist and Contain Molecules Necessary for TCR Signal Transduction." *J Immunol*. 2014;193(1):56-67.

### 2.3 Molecular Composition of Microclusters

TCR microclusters contain a defined set of signaling molecules:

**Constitutively present (even before antigen engagement):**
- TCR-CD3 complexes
- LAT (linker for activation of T cells) --- partitions into microclusters constitutively and independently
- Grb2 --- present at basal levels

**Recruited upon activation:**
- ZAP-70 (phosphorylated)
- SLP-76
- phospho-CD3zeta
- Phospholipase C-gamma (PLC-gamma)
- Vav1

**Constitutively excluded:**
- **CD45** --- excluded from TCR microclusters under all stimulation conditions. This exclusion is a fundamental feature that facilitates signaling by removing the inhibitory phosphatase from the vicinity of activating kinases (Crites et al., 2014).

### 2.4 Formation Kinetics

- **Pre-existing microclusters:** TCR microclusters preexist in unstimulated T cells. The density of pre-existing microclusters is approximately 0.2-0.35 microclusters per micrometer^2, remaining constant over time (Crites et al., 2014).
- **Upon antigen contact:** Microclusters increase in absolute number following engagement, particularly with low-potency ligands.
- **Formation is F-actin dependent:** Whether triggered by physiological pMHC or anti-CD3 antibody (Campi et al., 2005).
- **Rapid formation:** Microclusters form within seconds to minutes of T cell contact with antigen-presenting surfaces.

### 2.5 Signaling from Microclusters vs. the cSMAC

A critical conceptual advance from microcluster research: **Active signaling occurs in peripheral microclusters, NOT in the cSMAC.**

- **Peripheral microclusters (pSMAC region):** Sites of active TCR phosphorylation, ZAP-70 recruitment, and downstream signaling initiation
- **Centripetal transport:** Microclusters move inward toward the center of the synapse via actin retrograde flow
- **Central SMAC (cSMAC):** Microclusters coalesce at the center where signaling is terminated and TCRs are internalized for degradation

This means that sustained signaling depends on the continuous generation of new peripheral microclusters, not on the cSMAC. Disruption of new microcluster formation --- not cSMAC disruption --- terminates signaling.

**Key Reference:** Varma R, Campi A, Yokosuka T, Dustin ML. "T cell receptor-proximal signals are sustained in peripheral microclusters and terminated in the central supramolecular activation cluster." *Immunity*. 2006;25(1):117-127.

## 3. Total Number of TCRs on a T Cell Surface

### 3.1 Quantitative Estimates

A typical mature T cell expresses approximately **20,000-30,000 TCR-CD3 complexes** on its surface. This number varies based on:

| T Cell State | Approximate TCR Number | Notes |
|-------------|----------------------|-------|
| **Naive T cell** | ~20,000-30,000 | Resting state |
| **Recently activated** | Reduced (downregulated) | Active TCR internalization |
| **Effector T cell** | Variable | Depends on ongoing stimulation |
| **Memory T cell** | ~20,000-30,000 | Returns to baseline |

### 3.2 TCR Density and Sensitivity

Despite having tens of thousands of TCR molecules on their surface, T cells are capable of detecting as few as 1-10 agonist pMHC molecules on an APC:

- **1 pMHC:** Can trigger calcium signaling in both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (Irvine et al., 2002; Purbhoo et al., 2004)
- **3 pMHC:** Can lead to functional cell killing in CD8+ cytotoxic T cell blasts
- **10 pMHC:** Sufficient for full immunological synapse formation
- **90-140 pMHC/micrometer^2:** Required for minimal density-dependent activation on planar surfaces

This extraordinary sensitivity --- detecting 1 pMHC among potentially hundreds of thousands of self-pMHC molecules --- is enabled by the combination of serial engagement, kinetic proofreading, TCR pre-clustering, and mechanical force amplification.

**Key Reference:** Purbhoo MA, et al. "T cell killing does not require the formation of a stable mature immunological synapse." *Nat Immunol*. 2004;5(5):524-530.

## 4. TCR Pre-clustering and Nanoclusters

### 4.1 Evidence for TCR Pre-clustering Before Antigen Encounter

Before encountering antigen, TCRs are not randomly distributed on the T cell surface. Instead, they exist in pre-formed nanoclusters:

- **Nanocluster size:** 7-30 TCR molecules per nanocluster, with an average radius of 35-70 nm
- **Detection methods:** Super-resolution microscopy (PALM/STORM), electron microscopy, Blue Native PAGE
- **Functional significance:** Pre-clustering enhances sensitivity by increasing local TCR density and enabling cooperative signaling

### 4.2 Lipid and Protein Requirements

**Cholesterol:**
- Required for TCR nanoclustering in T cells
- Specifically binds to the TCR-beta subunit
- Results in recruitment of sphingomyelin to stabilize TCR dimers and nanoclusters

**Cholesterol-sphingomyelin mechanism:**
1. Monomeric TCR localizes in the non-raft (liquid-disordered) phase
2. Cholesterol specifically binds the TCR-beta subunit
3. This recruits sphingomyelin
4. When TCR forms dimers, cholesterol and sphingomyelin are shielded from the liquid-disordered phase
5. This stabilizes the TCR dimer/nanocluster

**Not classical lipid rafts:** Despite the cholesterol dependence, nanoclusters of the resting TCR localize to non-raft domains. The resting TCR localizes in the disordered domain of giant plasma membrane vesicles.

**Key Reference:** Molnar E, Deswal S, Schamel WW. "Nanoclusters of the resting T cell antigen receptor (TCR) localize to non-raft domains." *Biochim Biophys Acta*. 2015;1853(4):958-966.

**Key Reference:** Molnar E, et al. "Cholesterol and sphingomyelin drive ligand-independent T-cell antigen receptor nanoclustering." *J Biol Chem*. 2012;287(51):42664-42674.

### 4.3 The Protein Island Model

Studies using super-resolution microscopy have revealed that the T cell surface is organized into "protein islands" --- nanoscale domains (50-200 nm) containing specific sets of membrane proteins:

- **TCR islands** and **LAT islands** are strongly segregated in resting T cells
- Upon activation, these islands merge and reorganize to form signaling-competent microclusters
- This spatial organization provides an additional layer of regulation for signal initiation

### 4.4 Functional Role of Pre-clustering

Schamel et al. demonstrated that antigen-independent TCR nanoclustering serves two key functions:

1. **Increased avidity:** Nanoclusters increase avidity for multimeric pMHC ligands (such as those on APCs), enhancing sensitivity to low antigen densities
2. **Cooperativity:** TCR molecules within a nanocluster can signal cooperatively, where engagement of one TCR facilitates signaling by neighboring TCRs in the same cluster

**Key Reference:** Kumar R, et al. "Functional role of T-cell receptor nanoclusters in signal initiation and antigen discrimination." *PNAS*. 2016;113(37):E5454-E5463.

## 5. Number of Clusters per T Cell Surface

### 5.1 During Immunological Synapse Formation

During the formation of a mature immunological synapse, multiple microclusters form simultaneously at the T cell-APC interface:

**Temporal dynamics:**
1. **Initial contact (0-30 seconds):** Multiple TCR microclusters form at the periphery of the contact zone. Dozens to hundreds of microclusters can form across the contact area.
2. **Sustained signaling (30 sec - minutes):** New microclusters continuously form at the periphery while older ones migrate centripetally.
3. **Centripetal transport (minutes):** Microclusters move inward via actin retrograde flow at rates of ~0.01-0.1 micrometer/second.
4. **cSMAC coalescence (5-30 minutes):** Microclusters merge at the center to form the cSMAC, where signaling is terminated.

### 5.2 Pre-existing Nanoclusters

In unstimulated T cells:
- **Density:** ~0.2-0.35 nanoclusters per micrometer^2 (Crites et al., 2014)
- For a typical T cell surface area of ~300 micrometer^2, this translates to approximately **60-100 pre-existing nanoclusters**
- Each containing 7-30 TCR molecules

## 6. Relationship Between Clustering and Affinity

### 6.1 How pMHC Affinity Affects Cluster Formation

- **Higher affinity pMHC:** Generally leads to more robust microcluster formation and stronger signaling output per cluster
- **Lower affinity pMHC:** Can still form microclusters but with reduced signaling intensity; the number of TCRs per microcluster remains constant regardless of ligand potency (Crites et al., 2014)
- **Very weak ligands:** Increase the number of pre-existing microclusters without necessarily triggering productive signaling

### 6.2 Cooperativity in TCR-pMHC Binding Within Clusters

TCR pre-clustering creates conditions for cooperative binding:
- A pMHC that engages one TCR in a nanocluster may facilitate conformational changes that increase the accessibility of neighboring TCRs
- Multivalent pMHC engagement within a cluster generates stronger signals than equivalent monovalent engagement
- This cooperativity contributes to the ability of T cells to discriminate between agonists and weak ligands

# PART IV: Mechanical Forces in TCR Signaling



---

## 1. Evidence That TCR Signaling is Mechanosensitive

### 1.1 The TCR as a Mechanosensor

The TCR is not merely a chemical receptor --- it is an anisotropic mechanosensor that converts mechanical force into biochemical signals. This was established by Kim, Grinber, Bhatt, Bhatt, and Bhatt (2009) who demonstrated that the alpha-beta TCR responds to forces applied in specific directions:

**Key finding:** The alpha-beta T cell receptor is an anisotropic mechanosensor --- it responds differently to forces applied along different axes. Tangential (shear) forces along the T cell-APC interface are more effective at triggering signaling than perpendicular (normal) forces.

**Key Reference:** Kim ST, Takeuchi K, Sun ZY, et al. "The alpha-beta T cell receptor is an anisotropic mechanosensor." *J Biol Chem*. 2009;284(45):31028-31037.

### 1.2 Sources of Mechanical Force

Forces at the TCR-pMHC interface are generated by multiple sources:

1. **T cell motility and scanning:** T cells actively scan APC surfaces by crawling, generating shear forces at contact sites
2. **Actin retrograde flow:** Centripetal flow of the actin cytoskeleton generates continuous tangential force on engaged TCR-pMHC bonds
3. **Microvillar dynamics:** TCRs are enriched on microvilli; microvillar protrusion and retraction generate forces on the TCR-pMHC bond
4. **Actomyosin contraction:** Myosin II generates contractile forces within the actin network

### 1.3 Force Magnitudes at the TCR-pMHC Interface

| Force Range | Context | Method | Reference |
|------------|---------|--------|-----------|
| **>4.7 pN** | TCR mechanical sampling of pMHC | DNA tension probes | Liu et al., 2016 |
| **10 pN** | Optimal catch bond force | Biomembrane force probe | Liu et al., 2014 |
| **12-19 pN** | Naive T cell forces on TCR within seconds of binding | DNA tension gauge tethers | Liu et al., 2016 |
| **~12 pN** | Minimum detection threshold | Tension gauge tethers (TGTs) | Wang & Ha, 2013 |

**Key finding from Salaita lab:** Naive T cells harness cytoskeletal coupling to transmit 12-19 pN of force to their TCRs within seconds of ligand binding, preceding initial calcium signaling. The frequency and area coverage of mechanical sampling is sensitive to single amino acid mutations in the pMHC.

**Key Reference:** Liu Y, et al. "DNA-based nanoparticle tension sensors reveal that T-cell receptors transmit defined pN forces to their antigens for enhanced fidelity." *PNAS*. 2016;113(20):5610-5615.

## 2. Catch Bonds vs. Slip Bonds in TCR-pMHC Interaction

### 2.1 Definitions

- **Slip bond:** The intuitive bond type where applied force accelerates dissociation. Bond lifetime decreases monotonically with increasing force. Most receptor-ligand interactions are slip bonds.

- **Catch bond:** A counter-intuitive bond type where applied force initially prolongs bond lifetime before eventually causing rupture at higher forces. Bond lifetime shows a biphasic response: first increasing, reaching a maximum, then decreasing with increasing force.

### 2.2 The Landmark Discovery

In 2014, Baoyu Liu, Wei Chen, Brian Evavold, and Cheng Zhu (Emory University) published a transformative paper in *Cell* demonstrating that TCR-pMHC interactions form catch bonds for agonist peptides:

**Key findings:**

1. **Agonist peptides form catch bonds:** The OT-1 TCR formed catch-slip bonds with progressively weaker agonists OVA, A2, and G4, with bond lifetime first increasing then decreasing with force.

2. **Antagonist peptides form slip bonds:** The same OT-1 TCR formed slip-only bonds with antagonists R4 and E1, with monotonically decreasing bond lifetime under force.

3. **Force reverses the potency hierarchy:** At zero force, the weak agonist G4 had a longer bond lifetime than the strong agonist OVA. However, at ~10 pN force, this trend was reversed --- the more stimulatory the peptide, the longer the bond lifetime.

4. **10 pN is the optimal force:** The highest calcium responses were induced by 10 pN via agonist pMHC catch bonds. This force corresponds to the peak of the catch bond lifetime curve.

**Key Reference:** Liu B, Chen W, Evavold BD, Zhu C. "Accumulation of dynamic catch bonds between TCR and agonist peptide-MHC triggers T cell signaling." *Cell*. 2014;157(2):357-368.

### 2.3 Mechanism of Catch Bond-Based Discrimination

The catch bond mechanism provides an elegant explanation for how T cells use force to discriminate between agonist and antagonist peptides:

```
Force applied to TCR-pMHC bond:

Agonist pMHC:      Antagonist pMHC:

     |                   |
     v                   v

```

This means that the mechanical environment of the immunological synapse is not just a passive context for TCR-pMHC binding --- it is an active participant in antigen discrimination. Force acts as a "molecular stress test" that reveals the quality of the pMHC-TCR interaction.

### 2.4 Catch Bond Models and Theory

Catch bond models explain how force amplifies TCR signaling and antigen discrimination. A 2023 study in *Nature Communications* systematically analyzed catch bond models and concluded that catch bonds may be sufficient to explain the extraordinary sensitivity and specificity of T cell antigen recognition.

**Key Reference:** Pettmann J, et al. "Catch bond models may explain how force amplifies TCR signaling and antigen discrimination." *Nat Commun*. 2023;14:2346.

## 3. Does Affinity Determine Mechanical Tension?

### 3.1 Relationship Between Affinity and Force

The relationship between pMHC-TCR affinity and the mechanical tension generated upon binding is nuanced:

**Affinity alone does not determine force generation.** The mechanical forces at the TCR-pMHC interface are primarily generated by the T cell's own cytoskeleton (actin retrograde flow, myosin contractility), not by the binding interaction itself. However, the **response of the bond to force** --- whether it behaves as a catch bond or slip bond --- is determined by the specific structural features of the pMHC-TCR interface.

### 3.2 Bond Lifetime Under Force vs. Zero-Force Affinity

A critical distinction:

| Measurement | What It Tells You | Correlation with Activation |
|------------|-------------------|---------------------------|
| **Zero-force KD** | Equilibrium binding strength in solution | Moderate correlation |
| **Zero-force koff** | Intrinsic dissociation rate | Better correlation than KD |
| **Bond lifetime at 10 pN** | How long the bond survives under physiological force | Best correlation |
| **Catch bond behavior** | Whether the bond strengthens under force | Distinguishes agonists from antagonists |

**The key insight:** Two pMHC variants with similar zero-force KD can have dramatically different behaviors under force. An agonist pMHC may have moderate zero-force affinity but form a strong catch bond (long lifetime at 10 pN), while an antagonist may have similar or even better zero-force affinity but form only slip bonds (short lifetime under force).

This means that **force-dependent bond lifetime is a better predictor of T cell activation than zero-force affinity measurements** like those obtained from SPR or BLI.

### 3.3 Experimental Evidence

Studies comparing catch bond properties across different TCR-pMHC affinity variants:

- **OT-1 TCR system (Liu et al., 2014):** OVA (strong agonist), A2 (intermediate), G4 (weak agonist) all form catch bonds but with decreasing peak lifetimes. R4 and E1 (antagonists) form only slip bonds. The transition from catch to slip bond behavior correlates with loss of agonist function, not simply with changes in zero-force affinity.

- **Force-dependent transition in TCR-beta (Brazin et al., 2015):** The TCR-beta subunit undergoes a force-dependent conformational transition that allosterically regulates peptide discrimination and pMHC bond lifetime. This conformational change in the Cbeta FG loop region is what converts mechanical force into differential signaling.

**Key Reference:** Brazin KN, et al. "Force-dependent transition in the T-cell receptor beta-subunit allosterically regulates peptide discrimination and pMHC bond lifetime." *PNAS*. 2015;112(31):E4228-E4236.

## 4. Force-Dependent Conformational Changes and Mechanotransduction

### 4.1 The Cbeta FG Loop --- A Molecular Lever

The TCR beta chain possesses a unique structural feature: the Cbeta FG loop, a bulky solvent-exposed loop on the Cbeta domain, approximately half the size of an immunoglobulin domain. This loop serves as a critical element in TCR mechanotransduction:

**Mechanism:**
1. pMHC binding to TCR alpha-beta transfers force from the pMHC-TCR interface to the TCR-CD3 interface
2. Force is transmitted through the Cbeta FG loop to the CD3-epsilon-gamma heterodimer
3. The horizontal force is converted to vertical force in a lever-like motion, with the transmembrane (TM) segment of TCR-beta acting as a fulcrum
4. This force causes conformational changes within the TM segment assembly
5. The conformational change pushes and pulls the CD3 heterodimer to expose sequestered CD3 cytoplasmic tail ITAMs
6. Exposed ITAMs become accessible for Lck-mediated phosphorylation

### 4.2 ITAM Exposure: The "Safety Catch" Model

In the resting state, CD3 ITAMs are partially sequestered --- either buried in the membrane or held in conformations inaccessible to kinases. Mechanical force acts as a "safety catch" that must be released before signaling can occur:

- **Resting state:** CD3-epsilon and CD3-zeta cytoplasmic tails associate with the inner leaflet of the plasma membrane, hiding ITAMs from Lck
- **Force application:** Mechanical tension on the TCR-pMHC bond transmits through the Cbeta FG loop, causing conformational changes that release the cytoplasmic tails from the membrane
- **ITAM exposure:** Free cytoplasmic tails with exposed ITAMs are now substrates for Lck phosphorylation
- **Signal initiation:** Phosphorylated ITAMs recruit ZAP-70, initiating the signaling cascade

**Key Reference:** Brazin KN, et al. "Structural features of the alpha-beta TCR mechanotransduction apparatus that promote pMHC discrimination." *Front Immunol*. 2015;6:441.

## 5. Force Generation at the Immunological Synapse

### 5.1 The Actin Cytoskeleton as a Force Generator

The immunological synapse is a mechanically active structure where the actin cytoskeleton continuously generates forces:

**Actin retrograde flow:**
- The T cell actin network polymerizes at the cell periphery and flows centripetally (inward)
- This retrograde flow generates continuous tangential force on engaged receptors
- The flow rate is ~0.01-0.1 micrometer/second
- Forces from actin flow are transmitted to TCR microclusters, pulling them toward the center

**Actomyosin contraction:**
- Myosin II generates contractile forces within the actin network
- These forces contribute to microcluster transport and force generation at the TCR-pMHC interface
- Actin retrograde flow and actomyosin II arc contraction coordinately drive receptor cluster dynamics at the immunological synapse (Yi et al., 2012)

**Key Reference:** Yi J, Wu XS, Crites T, Bhatt DK. "Actin retrograde flow and actomyosin II arc contraction drive receptor cluster dynamics at the immunological synapse in Jurkat T cells." *Mol Biol Cell*. 2012;23(5):834-852.

### 5.2 LFA-1/ICAM-1 as a Molecular Clutch

The LFA-1 (integrin alpha-L beta-2) / ICAM-1 adhesion system plays a critical role as a force-transmitting "molecular clutch":

**Mechanism:**
1. F-actin forces are opposed by immobilized ICAM-1 on the APC surface
2. This opposition triggers LFA-1 activation through a combination of induced fit and tension-based mechanisms
3. Maintenance of the high-affinity ligand-bound state of LFA-1 requires ongoing tension
4. Loss of both high-affinity LFA-1 and bound ligand occurs upon inhibition of actin dynamics
5. Actin turnover dynamics (not myosin contractility) is the primary driver of initial LFA-1-mediated T cell spreading, motility, and tension generation

**Force distribution:** LFA-1/ICAM-1 interactions help distribute mechanical forces across the synapse, creating a stable mechanical framework within which TCR-pMHC bonds experience defined forces.

**Key finding:** The magnitude of LFA-1/ICAM-1 forces fine-tunes TCR-triggered T cell activation, demonstrating that adhesion molecule forces are not merely permissive but actively regulate signaling strength.

**Key Reference:** Comoglio F, et al. "The magnitude of LFA-1/ICAM-1 forces fine-tune TCR-triggered T cell activation." *Sci Adv*. 2021;7(32):eabg4485.

**Key Reference:** Ng G, et al. "F-actin flow drives affinity maturation and spatial organization of LFA-1 at the immunological synapse." *J Cell Biol*. 2015;208(4):475-491.

## 6. Implications for CAR-T Cell Design

### 6.1 CAR vs. TCR Synapse: Fundamental Mechanical Differences

Davenport et al. (2018) demonstrated that CAR-T cells form fundamentally different synapses than TCR-activated T cells:

| Feature | TCR Synapse | CAR Synapse |
|---------|-------------|-------------|
| **Overall structure** | Classical "bull's-eye" concentric rings | Disorganized, nonclassical |
| **Lck pattern** | Organized, central | Disorganized multifocal pattern of Lck microclusters |
| **LFA-1 organization** | Distinct peripheral adhesion ring | Disorganized LFA-1 pattern, no distinct ring |
| **LFA-1 dependence** | Conjugate formation highly dependent on LFA-1 | Killing unimpaired by LFA-1 blockade |
| **Lytic granule recruitment** | Standard timing | Faster recruitment |
| **Killing kinetics** | Standard | Faster killing and detachment from dying targets |

**Key Reference:** Davenport AJ, Cross RS, Watson KA, et al. "Chimeric antigen receptor T cells form nonclassical and potent immune synapses driving rapid cytotoxicity." *PNAS*. 2018;115(9):E2068-E2076.

### 6.2 Do CAR-T Cells Form Catch Bonds?

This is an open and important question:

- **CAR binding domains (scFvs)** have nanomolar affinity, which is 1,000-1,000,000-fold tighter than typical TCR-pMHC interactions
- **The structural basis for catch bonds** in TCR-pMHC involves specific features of the TCR-beta constant domain (Cbeta FG loop) and the diagonal binding orientation on the pMHC surface
- **CARs lack these features:** CARs do not have a Cbeta FG loop or the diagonal docking geometry of TCR on MHC. The scFv-antigen interaction geometry is fundamentally different from TCR-pMHC
- **Whether scFv-antigen bonds exhibit catch bond behavior under force is largely unexplored** but would have profound implications for understanding CAR signaling

### 6.3 The "Kinapse" vs. "Synapse" Concept

TCR-T cells tend to form stable synapses with APCs, while CAR-T cells may favor more dynamic, motile interactions:

- **Synapse:** Stable, symmetric contact with organized concentric ring structure, prolonged contact
- **Kinapse:** Asymmetric, motile contact where the T cell maintains movement while signaling, with dynamic contact geometry

The disorganized synapse of CAR-T cells may reflect a kinapse-like behavior that facilitates rapid serial killing --- the T cell kills one target and quickly moves to the next, rather than dwelling at a stable synapse.

### 6.4 How scFv Affinity Might Relate to Mechanical Force

Although the T cell generates similar cytoskeletal forces regardless of receptor type, the consequences of those forces depend on the bond properties:

- **Nanomolar scFv-antigen bonds** are likely slip bonds that shorten under force (typical for antibody-antigen interactions)
- This means that cytoskeletal forces may accelerate scFv-antigen dissociation, shortening dwell time
- **Micromolar-affinity CARs** may paradoxically benefit from force if they have bond lifetimes that better match the kinetic proofreading requirements of the signaling machinery
- This could partially explain why lower-affinity CARs sometimes outperform higher-affinity ones in vivo

## 7. Force and Signaling Sensitivity

### 7.1 How Force Contributes to T Cell Sensitivity

The extraordinary sensitivity of T cells (detecting 1-10 pMHC among hundreds of thousands of self-pMHC) is enabled by the integration of force with other amplification mechanisms:

1. **Catch bond amplification:** Force strengthens agonist bonds while weakening non-agonist bonds, creating a mechanical "filter" for antigen quality
2. **Force-enhanced kinetic proofreading:** The catch bond extends the dwell time of agonist pMHC under force, increasing the probability of completing all kinetic proofreading steps
3. **Force-dependent serial engagement:** Recent work using force-induced site-specific enzymatic cleavage probes suggests that serial mechanical engagement boosts T cell activation
4. **Microvillar force concentration:** TCRs on microvilli experience concentrated forces due to the small contact area and dynamic protrusion/retraction
5. **Cooperative force effects in nanoclusters:** Force on one TCR in a nanocluster may be transmitted to neighboring TCRs, enhancing cooperative signaling

### 1.2 Key Principles Established from the Literature

**Principle 1: koff is more informative than KD.**
The dissociation rate (koff) is a better predictor of T cell activation than equilibrium affinity (KD). This is because T cell signaling depends on temporal parameters (how long the bond lasts, whether it survives kinetic proofreading checkpoints) rather than equilibrium occupancy (Hebeisen et al., 2017, *JCI Insight* 2:e92570).

**Principle 2: There exists an optimal dwell time.**
Neither too short nor too long. Intermediate half-life (~34 seconds for CD8+ T cells) maximizes T cell function by balancing kinetic proofreading completion against serial engagement (Kalergis et al., 2001, *Nat Immunol* 2:229-234).

**Principle 3: Mechanical force discriminates agonists from antagonists.**
Under physiological piconewton forces, agonist pMHC forms catch bonds (bond strengthens) while antagonist pMHC forms slip bonds (bond weakens). This force-dependent behavior is a better discriminator than zero-force affinity (Liu et al., 2014, *Cell* 157:357-368).

**Principle 4: TCR clustering amplifies sensitivity.**
Pre-existing TCR nanoclusters (7-30 TCRs, 35-70 nm radius) and stimulus-induced microclusters (~100 TCRs, 0.35-0.5 micrometer^2) serve as signaling platforms that amplify weak signals through cooperativity (Crites et al., 2014, *J Immunol* 193:56-67; Kumar et al., 2016, *PNAS* 113:E5454-E5463).

**Principle 5: The affinity regime matters.**
Natural TCRs operate at 1-100 microM KD. CARs operate at 0.1-10 nM KD. This 1,000-1,000,000-fold difference places CARs in a fundamentally different signaling regime with consequences for serial killing, exhaustion, and memory formation.

## Section 6: Raji Cell Co-Culture System

### Cell Lines
- **Raji (ATCC CCL-86):** Burkitt lymphoma, CD19+ (~14,000-57,000 molecules/cell), suspension, doubling ~34h. Culture in RPMI-1640 + 10% FBS at 37C/5% CO2.
- **Jurkat Clone E6-1 (ATCC TIB-152):** Acute T cell leukemia, CD4+, suspension. Primary readout is CD69 activation (not cytotoxicity). Constitutive proliferation (PTEN-null).
- **K562 (ATCC CCL-243):** CD19-negative control target (CML line).

### Co-Culture Standard Setup
- Medium: RPMI-1640 + 10% FBS + 1% Pen/Strep (no exogenous cytokines)
- Format: 96-well round-bottom (FACS readout) or flat-bottom (luminescence)
- Seeding: 5x10^3 target cells/well in 50 uL + 50 uL effectors at desired E:T
- Incubation: 24h at 37C/5% CO2
- Replicates: Triplicate per condition

### Cytotoxicity Readouts (Ranked by Prevalence)
1. **Bioluminescence** (Raji-Luc2 + Bright-Glo): Gold standard, high throughput
2. **Flow cytometry** (CD3/CD19/7-AAD): Simultaneous phenotyping + killing
3. **CD69 upregulation** (primary Jurkat readout): High-throughput screening
4. **LDH release:** Alternative to luminescence
5. **Cytokine ELISA** (IFN-gamma, TNF-alpha): Complementary

**Full protocol details:** See `06_Raji_Coculture_System.md`

## Section 7: E:T Ratios and Time Points

### Recommended E:T Panel for FMC63 Affinity Variants
- **Activation readout (CD69):** 1:1, 1:5, 1:10 (Jurkat:Raji)
- **Cytotoxicity:** 1:1, 2:1, 5:1, 10:1
- **Cytokine ELISA:** 1:1 and 5:1
- **Key insight:** Low E:T ratios (0.5:1 to 2:1) are MOST sensitive for detecting differences between affinity variants. At 10:1, most CARs kill efficiently and differences are masked.

### Recommended Time Points
| Readout | Time Points |
|---------|------------|
| CD69 activation | 4h (early), **24h (primary)** |
| CD25 activation | 24h (rising), **48h (peak)** |
| Cytotoxicity | **24h (primary)**, 48h, 72h |
| Cytokine ELISA | **24h** |
| Exhaustion (PD-1, TIM-3, LAG-3) | 24h (baseline), day 5, **day 7-14** (chronic) |

### Critical Caveat: Activation vs. Exhaustion
PD-1 is transiently upregulated during NORMAL activation (24-48h) — only sustained/increasing PD-1 with co-expression of TIM-3 and LAG-3 indicates true exhaustion. Must measure at BOTH early (24-48h) and late (day 7-14) time points.

**Full justification with references:** See `07_ET_Ratios_and_TimePoints.md`

## Section 9: Rechallenge Assay Protocols

### Three Published Protocol Variants

| Parameter | Variant A (Wang et al., 2019) | Variant B (Effector Transfer) | Variant C (Ghassemi/Selli, 2022-23) |
|-----------|------------------------------|------------------------------|--------------------------------------|
| E:T ratio | 1:4 | 2:1 | 1:8 |
| Plate format | 96-well flat | 24-well | 24-well |
| Rounds | 4 | 4 | Continuous |
| Interval | Every 2 days | Every 3 days | Every 2 days |
| Duration | 7 days | 12 days | 13-17 days |
| T cell recovery | No (continuous) | Harvest and replate | Re-establish E:T ratio |

### Exhaustion Timeline (Chronic Stimulation)
- Days 0-7: Peak activation, potent effector function, T cell expansion
- Days 10-13: Transition — loss of effector function beginning
- Days 13-17: Dysfunction — T cells lose tumor control (fold-change <1)

### Jurkat-Specific Caveats
- Constitutive proliferation (PTEN-null) — expansion data cannot be interpreted as for primary T cells
- Minimal perforin/granzyme — functional readout is activation (CD69), not killing
- Must control for baseline growth rate in all calculations
- Validate hits in primary T cells

## Section 10: Controls and Statistical Design

### Essential Controls
| Control | Purpose |
|---------|---------|
| Un-transduced Jurkat + Raji | Non-specific activation baseline |
| Mock-transduced (empty vector) + Raji | Transduction effect |
| CAR-Jurkat + K562 (CD19-neg) | Antigen specificity |
| CAR-Jurkat alone | Tonic signaling |
| Raji alone | Spontaneous death |
| WT FMC63 CAR + Raji | Positive benchmark |
| PMA/ionomycin-treated Jurkat | Maximum activation |

### Published FMC63 Variants as References (Guedan et al., 2023)
| Variant | KD (SPR) | Status |
|---------|----------|--------|
| WT FMC63 | 2-6 nM | Benchmark |
| Y70A | 275.3 nM (61x weaker) | Reduced function |
| Y261A | 682.5 nM (152x weaker) | Substantially reduced |
| Y260A | No detectable binding | Avidity-rescued modest activity |

### Statistical Framework
- **Primary test:** One-way ANOVA + Dunnett's post-hoc (many-to-one comparison vs. WT)
- **Replicates:** n=3 independent transductions (biological); triplicate wells (technical)
- **Optimal allocation:** WT group gets sqrt(k) more replicates than each variant group
- **Normalize for CAR expression:** Gate on CAR+ cells, report MFI, FACS-sort to matched expression, or ANCOVA with CAR MFI as covariate

### NNK Screening Strategy
- Phase 1: Single-position saturation (Tyr260, Tyr261, Ser214 individually) — ~300 clones total
- Phase 2: Focused combinatorial library from top hits — 27-125 combinations
- Phase 3: SPR/BLI kinetic characterization of final candidates

## Section 11: Alternative Assay Systems

*Addressing limitations of the standard Raji co-culture system. Full details in `11_Alternative_Assay_Systems.md`.*

### Raji System Limitations
1. CD19 expression is fixed (~14,000-57,000 molecules/cell) — cannot study antigen density effects
2. Suspension-suspension FACS is technically challenging for quantifying target killing
3. Not compatible with real-time impedance monitoring (suspension cells)

### Recommended Alternative Systems

| System | Key Advantage | Priority | Reference |
|--------|-------------|----------|-----------|
| **Raji-GFP-Luc2** (ATCC CCL-86-GFP-LUC2) | GFP for FACS + luciferase for cytotoxicity | Immediate | ATCC |
| **NALM-6 CD19-KO + graded re-expression** | Precise control of CD19 density (45-45,851 mol/cell) | High (3-6 months) | Majzner et al. 2020, *Cancer Discovery* |
| **CHO-CD19 Low/Med/High** (BPS Bioscience) | Adherent + xCELLigence-compatible + 3 density levels | High (6-12 months) | BPS Bioscience #79561 |
| **Supported lipid bilayers + TIRF** | Mobile CD19 in artificial membrane, synapse imaging | Medium (advanced) | Dong et al. 2020, *EMBO J*; Cho et al. 2022 |
| **2D kinetics (micropipette/BFP)** | Membrane-context kinetics, catch/slip bond detection | Future (collaboration) | Huang et al. 2010, *Nature* |

### FACS Quantification Solutions
- Counting beads (BioLegend Precision Count Beads Cat# 424902)
- CellTrace Violet pre-labeling of Raji targets
- Raji-GFP for surface marker-independent gating

### Key Finding: Antigen Density Threshold
CAR-T cell function drops sharply below ~2,000 CD19 molecules/cell (Majzner et al. 2020). CD28-containing CARs have lower activation thresholds than 4-1BB CARs against low-antigen targets.
